## Desk evidence

### Evidence — the category already solves “digitize the closet”

Whering, Stylebook, Acloset, Indyx, and Cladwell all publicly describe a closet of the person’s own clothes plus some form of outfit help. Sources and dates are in `01-product-analysis/competitive-analysis.md`, accessed 22 October 2026.

**Interpretation:** A generic closet grid is not a differentiated product. The case study has to be specific about privacy, explanation, and the manual path.

**Design implication:** Suggestions show their factors. Photos are not scanned. There is no feed and no shop. Packing names the outfits that share an item.

### Evidence — non-photo capture already exists

Stylebook’s FAQ lists five ways to add clothes without photos. Cladwell’s site says most people never take a photo and can pick from a catalog or paste a link. Both accessed 22 October 2026.

**Interpretation:** “Add without a photo” is a known expectation, not a new invention.

**Design implication:** Rewear’s manual form is still required by the PRD, and it asks only for the fields the matcher uses. It does not claim to be the first manual closet.

### Evidence — weather and location are a real product direction elsewhere

Whering’s Play listing (updated 5 October 2026) says the planner suggests outfits from the wardrobe and the weather, and that trips can be planned by changing location. Acloset’s 14 October 2026 note describes weather widgets.

**Interpretation:** Rewear’s manual weather preference will feel less magical than those products. That is a privacy trade, not an oversight.

**Design implication:** The outfit form says weather is a preference and that location is not collected.

### Evidence — competitor price facts that were actually checked

- Stylebook FAQ: $4.99 once in the US.
- Cladwell web pricing: Your Stylist at $9.99 a month or $79.99 a year after a 7-day trial for new users. Free closet without a card.
- Indyx’s own comparison: lookbooks from a stylist at $150. Insider’s monthly price was not on that page.
- Acloset’s listing: free up to 100 items, then a subscription. Dollar amounts on Indyx’s page are a competitor’s account.

**Design implication:** The prototype has no price. The case study does not invent one.

## Hypotheses the design is using

These are **not findings**.

| ID | Hypothesis | Design implication already taken | To validate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| H1 | Morning friction is coordination and memory, not a lack of clothes. | Grid, search, outfit as the saved object | Interview stories |
| H2 | People can tag color, season, and dressiness if the form is short. | Four required tags plus name | Add-item task |
| H3 | An explained average match is more trustworthy than a silent “AI outfit.” | Factor list, dismiss, manual builder | Whether people read the factors |
| H4 | Trip packing is outfit-first for at least some travelers. | Packing list is the union of outfits | Trip stories. If false, add extras. |
| H5 | A lecture about consumption would be rejected. | Banned tone. No impact counter. | Language reactions in the usability test |
| H6 | Five items is enough to attempt the PRD activation metric, and 12 sample items are enough to demo. | Sample set of 12. Test still asks for five adds. | Timed test |

## What must not be written into the case study later

- Composite quotes.
- “5 out of 6 users” unless there were 6 sessions and the notes show it.
- A changed success percentage that was not measured.

The portfolio narrative has to be able to say “validation planned” without embarrassment. That is the accurate status.
